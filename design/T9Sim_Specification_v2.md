## 1.1 What the generator emits

- One master parquet 𝒟^full (`auctions.parquet`; 55 columns, always, the five `la2_*` columns from dependency #1 and `bid_density` from dependency #6 among them; per-row availability in 1.5).
- Four censored views C1-C4 (the tables 𝒟^{obs,k}) derived from it. The master carries every column on every row: A, B, C, D families (observables), E, F, G, H families (outcome labels; H1 floor and H2 own bid are observed inputs), plus pool variables (latents) and estimands.

## 1.2 Market setting

**Market and calibration.** Tier-1 US mobile-gaming market, calibrated to US 2025 benchmarks, priced in USD. iPinYou (2013, China) supplies empirical distribution *shapes* for a subset of features; price *levels* are rescaled to 2025 US mobile eCPM. Region/city codes are anonymised integers with a canonical city → region hierarchy (no real names).

**Inventory and genre.** Every auction is mobile in-app inventory in a gaming app (publisher = a game). One genre taxonomy 𝒢 = {casual, strategy, rpg, hypercasual} (set literals take braces throughout, Symbol Plan 2.11) spans user interest, host-app category and advertised genre (real UA also runs on non-gaming inventory; a declared simplification).

**Competition.** Not gaming-only: it is modelled structurally as 3 gaming + 5 non-gaming rival archetypes (section 2.5).

**Auction format.** All auctions are **first-price** (2025 mobile in-app mediation: AppLovin MAX, Unity LevelPlay, AdMob). iPinYou's second-price `paying_price` is reused as the clearing-price shape (revenue equivalence); bid-shading dynamics are abstracted away.

**Attribution and user history.** Single-touch, with no cross-auction user history: a user's latents (LU1-LU6) and Section-A features are shared across all that user's auctions; only per-auction quantities vary.

## 1.3 Naming note

Plain C1-C4 always means the experimental conditions (C1 = DSP, C2 = DSP+MMP, C3 = DSP+SSP, C4 = all), never the campaign columns `C1`-`C4`. Extensions (Appendix M9): the labels E1-E5 in the rival-market material (sections 2.5 and 2.6) name graph edges, never the click columns `E1`/`E2`. The fourth Tier-1 head is ℓ, written 𝔼[spend | payer] and 𝔼[ltv | payer] interchangeably: one estimand, two conventional names (spend = LTV = G2).

## 1.4 Observability statuses

Every column has exactly one **primary** status. The test for *latent* is a single question: does the variable ever appear in the data the learner sees? It is not whether the generator knows its value (the generator knows everything).

| Status | Columns / variables | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Latent (ground-truth) parameters** | LU1-LU6, LA1-LA2, LC1-LC2, LR1-LR6 | Generative parameters: they shape the draws but are never columns in any view, and no model infers them. LU/LA/LC are master columns (dropped from views); LR1-LR6 are generator-internal (1.7). |
| **Latent auction variable** | LU7 `competing_bid` | A hidden per-auction draw (not a pool parameter), never a column in any view. On lost-but-sold rows in C3/C4 it leaks through `H4 = u_i`: that leak **is** the SSP signal. `u_i = max` over rival bids (2.5). |
| **Observed features** | A1-A5, B1-B6, `C1`-`C4`, D1-D3, H1 (floor), H2 (own bid) | Visible columns; model inputs in every condition. |
| **Outcome labels** | E1-E2, F1-F2, G1-G4, H3, H4, H9 | Random draws on **all** master rows, observed or censored per condition and row (1.5): H3 everywhere; E/F/G on won rows (C1/C3) or all rows (C2/C4); H4, H9 in C3/C4 only. |

Tier-1 estimates the funnel quantities, Tier-2 estimates F_{u\|x}(b), from visible features. |

Censoring is not latent status: it withholds an *observable* label from specific conditions and rows, and the C1-C4 comparison rests entirely on it. Admissibility follows: the five estimands, `lu7_competing_bid` and `la2_audience_shares` are never model features in any condition (nor in the oracle-features diagnostic).

## 1.5 Censoring map: the C1-C4 views

One master table 𝒟^full; four views 𝒟^{obs,k}. Identical rows and temporal split in every condition (split days 1-20 / 21-24 / 25-28 BEFORE censoring); only label visibility differs. Views are masks, never copies.

| Condition | Layers | E/F/G (click, install, payer, LTV) | H4 winning price | H9 rival count | Always visible |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C1** | DSP only | **won rows only** (standard postbacks) | hidden | hidden | A, B, `C`, D, H1, H2, H3 |
| **C2** | DSP + MMP | **all rows** (cross-network attribution) | hidden | hidden | A, B, `C`, D, H1, H2, H3 |
| **C3** | DSP + SSP | **won rows only** | **all rows** (NaN only where unsold) | **all rows** | A, B, `C`, D, H1, H2, H3 |
| **C4** | DSP + MMP + SSP | **all rows** | **all rows** | **all rows** | A, B, `C`, D, H1, H2, H3 |

`user_id` is master-lineage only (dropped from every view; user/device ids carry no signal under single-touch).

**NaN semantics.** Censored cells are **NaN** in the view, distinct from `-1` (an *observed* no-event timestamp on E2/F2) and from `0` labels; this applies to all of E1-E2, F1-F2, G1-G4 on masked rows. "Hidden" H4/H9 in C1/C2 are all-NaN columns (kept, not dropped, so all views share one column schema). *In the symbols of 2.7, a cell censored by 𝒞^k is ∅ and a quantity that does not exist on the row is ⊥; the word NaN is kept in prose and the symbols are used in the laws.*

**Real-world mapping (MMP).** Every DSP gets won-row postbacks (C1/C3). What MMP adds is attribution on the auctions the DSP *lost*: its tracking links and in-game SDK record clicks, installs and spend whoever won (C2/C4). Those lost rows are systematically the high-value impressions (the DSP's biased view).

**Real-world mapping (SSP).** An SSP sees every auction it runs: winning prices on losses (H4) and realized competition density (H9) are exactly what SSP ownership reveals and a lone DSP cannot observe.

**Counterfactual simplification (declared).** Lost-row E/F/G represent what the attribution chain would have recorded had our ad been served; the competitor's creative is not modelled. The outcome formulas never depend on `won`, so this counterfactual is well-defined within the model.

**What each lift measures.**

- **C1→C2**: value of cross-network attribution. Same rows, unbiased funnel labels; corrects the DSP's biased view (selection bias).
- **C1→C3**: value of price visibility. Lost-row winning prices and competition density fingerprint hidden rival structure; Tier-2 win-curve precision.
- **C2→C4**: marginal SSP on top of full attribution.
- **C3→C4**: marginal MMP on top of price visibility.

None of the dependency edges confounds the C1-C4 comparison: #1-#5 are marginal-preserving and act identically across views, and the rival pool touches the views only through H9 (SSP-only) and H4's lost-row values.

**Per-row availability (master table).** The master is never NaN-censored: the only NaN is H4 on unsold rows (no winning price exists); E2/F2 use -1 for "no event". All won/lost-conditional hiding happens in the condition views (this section), never in generation.

## 1.8 Per-family notes

- **A (user).** Root features of the user pool: region/city from iPinYou, os/os_version/device_type inferred rather than externally sourced (open item O3 3a); A4 nested on A3. The archetype tilts on A3 and A5 are wired in v2 (open item O1), so both are drawn from a raked archetype-conditional table and their marginals survive. Their tilt strengths were settled at open item O3 3b, one dial per table with tau = 0.13 on both, route `inferred`.
- **B (app context).** B1-B2 from the app pool, B3-B6 per-auction. B4/B5 are an IAB size pair conditional on format (via `_size`); category quota from Sensor Tower and Newzoo; exchange shares from US 2025 mediation.
- **C (campaign).** Advertiser ids anchored to iPinYou `advertiser_id`. Pool chain C3 → C1 → C2 (a campaign belongs to an advertiser with a scale tier); C4 is a campaign property. Frequency follows `advertiser_scale` via `sample_weight`.
- **D (time).** D2 and D3 drawn first; D1 assembled from them plus the week D4 (generator-internal). Time-structure scoping in 2.6 (edge #5).
- **E/F/G (funnel).** Generated on all rows (lost rows = the counterfactual, 1.5); the chain gates deterministically (no click → no install → no payer → zero spend). Timestamps use -1 for no-event.
- **H (market).** H5-H8 unused (the master jumps H4 to H9). H4 and H9 are SSP-exclusive: H3 (win/loss) is visible in all conditions; C3/C4 add the loss price (`H4 = u_i`) and competition density (`H9 = N`, 1-8 per auction).
- **LU/LA/LC (pool latents).** Withheld as columns. User latents are effectively unrecoverable (each user appears in only ≈ tens of rows); app and campaign latents are partly recoverable from `app_id`/`campaign_id` (constant across an entity's rows), which is realistic and does not confound the DSP/MMP/SSP comparison since those ids are visible in all of C1-C4.
- **LR (rival latents).** The complete family LR1-LR6, and the footprint rule that governs its observability, are specified in section 2.5.
- **Estimands.** The Tier-1 models estimate `p_click`, `p_install`, `p_payer`, `e_ltv` from visible features (the four heads are Pr(click), Pr(install | click), Pr(payer | install) and 𝔼[spend | payer]), and the bidder reconstructs an estimate of v^⋆ by multiplying them. Tier-2 estimand: F_{u|x}(b).

### What a node carries

A node's type is one field of several. In full, a node holds 4 groups, and the order below is the
order they depend on each other: a law cannot be stated until the parents are known, and what is
emitted cannot be known until the law exists.

**1. Identity, what it is**

- `id`, `family`, the generation stage it belongs to
- `role`: latent · observable · auxiliary · estimand · outcome

**2. Position, where it sits in the network**

- `parents`
- `plate`: per user, app, campaign, advertiser, rival, auction, or auction × rival. The last is the k plate: `participate_k` and `b_k` are drawn K = 8 times per auction row and reduce to auction scope through LU7 and H9. How often the node is drawn, and
  what it is held constant across. `LU1` has plate `user`, so it is drawn once per user and every
  auction that user appears in reuses it
- `edge_group`: its random-number stream. Each family draws from its own stream, seeded from the run
  seed and a hash of the stream name, so adding a node changes that stream's draws and leaves every
  other node's numbers untouched

**3. Law, how its value is produced.** Four levels, abstract to concrete.

| Level | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Type** | T1 calibrated draw · T2 probability table · T3 conditional distribution · T4 deterministic formula |
| **Statement** | the law in words, "Beta(a, b) from a parameter table keyed by archetype" |
| **Parameters** | the actual numbers, a and b per archetype |
| **Constraints** | R1 to R10, the rakes and identities the result must satisfy |

**4. Emission, what leaves the node**

- `kind`: column or internal
- `columns`
- `observability` per view C1 to C4

**Why `role` sits in Identity rather than Emission**, though it looks output-shaped: role largely
*determines* observability. A latent role is latent in all 4 views; an outcome role is won-rows-only
in C1 and C3. Observability is downstream of role, so role is the more primitive fact.

### The network these nodes form

All 78 are nodes of **one directed acyclic graph** with 115 edges, and there is exactly **one kind of
edge**: parent to child. The 4 types are not 4 kinds of relationship. They describe how a node
computes **its own** value, given whatever its parents happen to be.

That distinction settles the question a multi-parent node raises. `H2 bid_price` is

    ζ^global · ζ^cpa · q_i^slot · α_i^ease · ℓ̂^cat · ζ^shade · η_i,   η_i ∼ LogNormal(0, σ^explore)

which reads as 6 deterministic factors and one random draw, and looks like T4 and T3 stitched
together. It is one T3: a single lognormal whose location parameter happens to be a product of 6
things. The deterministic part is **inside** the parameter, not beside it. A node's parents may be of
any types in any mix; the node itself has one law and one type. See "Can a variable be a mixed type"
below for the 3 moves that resolve every apparent exception.

T4 is still a conditional distribution, not an exception to the network: it places probability 1 on
the single value its formula returns and 0 elsewhere. A point mass is a distribution, so the graph
remains a proper Bayesian network throughout.

### Where each layer actually lives

The 4 groups are not stored together, and knowing which file holds which is the difference between a
node that is complete and one that only looks complete.

| Group | Where it lives | How it gets there |
|---|---|---|
| Identity, Position, Law **type**, Emission | `config/graph.yaml` | generated from the node register by `tools/build_graph.py` |
| Law **statement**, Constraints | the node register | hand-written |
| **Parameters**, design constants | `config/market.yaml` and `config/tables.yaml` | hand-written, each as `{value, route, source}` |
| **Parameters**, run settings | `config/profiles.yaml` — rho, seeds, scales, `block_rows`, the four pool sizes | hand-written, same wrapper |
| **Parameters**, solved constants | `config/calibrated.yaml` | written by the step-0 calibration pass, never hand-set |

**Parameters have a different home from everything else about a node.** That is the structural reason
a node can be fully declared and still undrawable: `graph.yaml` can say LU2 is a T3 drawn per user
with parent LU1, the register can say its law is a Beta keyed by archetype, and no file states what
a and b are for a whale. Structurally complete, numerically empty.

This was not hypothetical. On 15 August 2026 the gap detector was upgraded to report keys the
register names that the config has no home for, and it found 22, of which about 15 were exactly this:
declared nodes whose law parameters existed nowhere. All are now filled. Two guards came out of it,
and both are worth keeping in mind when adding a node:

- The register's Source column names the settings key that **holds** each value, not only where the
  value came from. A missing parameter now appears as a broken pointer rather than as silence.
- `tools/gate_report.py` reports 3 classes at every gate, not one: `HOLE` a key whose value no
  document supplies, `UNSOURCED` a value with no route or source, and `UNDECLARED` a key the register
  names with no home in the config.

**One thing still held in code rather than in a document.** `plate` and `edge_group` are not stated
per node anywhere in the register. `build_graph.py` infers both from the family heading through a
lookup table inside the script. Two structural properties of every node therefore live in code, which
is the one place the register is not yet the single source of truth. Recorded at
`t9v2/docs/SPEC_GAPS.md`.

### Assignment flowchart in words

Ask in order. Every path ends in exactly one type.

1. Given its parents, is the value a fixed function with no random draw? Yes → T4. Add a plate block if it reduces over indexed copies. Add `ground_truth: true` if the benchmark scores against it.
2. Does its randomness live in a helper the code draws first (an index, a shape resample, a joint)? Yes → register the hidden draw as its own node, restart at step 1 for the helper; the emitted variable becomes a T4 lookup or split.
3. Does it have no parents, and is its law one draw from a sourced table or named distribution? Yes → T1. A joint table with an empty per-row parent set (`pair_idx`) is not one such draw and falls through to step 4. Remember the fixed-index rule: a table dimension is not a parent.
4. Is the child categorical and is the law a stated pmf per parent cell (or a raked joint table with no per-row parents)? Yes → T2. Add a rake block only where IPF preserves a calibrated marginal. Implicit uniform-over-members index draws stay in step 5.
5. Otherwise → T3. Absorb gates as point-mass branches. A deterministic core times noise puts the core in the location parameter.

### Flags, dials, solved constants, constraints, scope

- **There are no variants. The 6 dependencies are always on.** v1 carried an edge flag per dependency and every node had to declare a second law for the switched-off case. v2 drops that. Pairing, exposure, os_spend, payer_timing, hour and `rival_pool` are fixed properties of the design, not dials, so every node has exactly one law and type is a property of the node alone. See open item O11.
- **A dial has exactly one home**, holding a `{value, route, source}` wrapper. A dial that is a property of the design sits in its node's law block. A dial that is a property of a run sits in the profile. rho is a run setting, so its one home is `profiles.yaml`, not b_k's law block (open item O3 3d). The loader's duplicate-key check fails on a second copy, which is what stops the script-versus-config gap v1 had.
- **Solved constants are a third class, not nodes and not free dials**: k_global (bisection to win rate 0.30), z_mu and z_sigma (warm-up standardisation), the five autocal knobs. Each declares its solver, target and tolerance; the gate is that re-solving reproduces the target.
- **Constraints R1–R10 attach to nodes**, not to types, as machine-checkable asserts. A deliberate absence is declared, not silent: the rival pool's non-rake is `constraint: none, backstop: [k_global pin R7, validation gates]`.
- **Scope rule (keeps the graph acyclic).** Pool and entity scope quantities enter auction scope only through lookups, and a lookup can never close a cycle. Build-time table inputs (LA2 into the pair_idx table, LC2 into the c_idx tilt) are parameter edges, not sampling parents. Rake targets (the archetype mix in R2) are constraints, not parents.

### Provenance routes, and why they are not the node types

Every constant in the settings files carries a `{value, route, source}` wrapper. **Route** records where the number came from. It is a property of a single leaf value, not of a node, and it is independent of the node type. A node usually owns several leaves and they may take different routes.

| Route | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `quoted` | computed directly from a number cited outside the project |
| `auto-calibrated` | solved so a simulator aggregate hits a cited target |
| `inferred` | documented judgement, flagged and sensitivity-tested |
| `specified` | inherited from a project design document, cited by file and line |

`specified` is new in v2. It is kept apart from `quoted` so that a value inherited from our own documents never reads as an externally evidenced one. `source` must resolve to a location a checker can open, not to prose.

The 2 axes cross freely. LU5 shows it in one node. Three of its four archetype locations are `auto-calibrated` against the whale-share target, casual's is the unit reference the other three are measured from, every spread is an `inferred` judgement, and the inactive zero is `specified` as an exact identity. The dollar anchor is not among them, since it lives in the base LTV curve at `ltv.base_median_usd` and is never counted twice. Route belongs to the leaf, not to the node.

Two consequences follow from the axes being independent. A leaf can need a route and have no node type, because settings such as the XGBoost parameters, the split days and `repro_tolerance` are not variables in the network. A node can have a type and no route at all, because a T4 formula that is a pure function of its parents has no constant to cite.

**The v1 baseline this replaces.** v1 wraps 127 of 588 leaves, of which 94 are `inferred`, 19 `quoted`, 9 `auto-calibrated` and 5 untagged. `bn_cpts.yaml`, `profiles.yaml` and `validation.yaml` carry no wrappers at all, and 106 of `benchmarks.yaml`'s bare leaves are numbers rather than structural labels. v2 requires a wrapper on every leaf that is not a structural label, which is what the `specified` column absorbs.

**A worked case for why the source must be checkable.** The node register attributes C4's genre mix to AppsFlyer 2026 and SocialPeta, which reads as externally sourced, while `ad_genre_mix.casual` in `benchmarks.yaml` is tagged `inferred` with the note "BM-bounded". The 2 documents disagree about how well evidenced the same number is, and no tool can detect that while `source` is free text.

### Can a variable be a mixed type

No. There is no mixed type and none is needed. Every apparently mixed law resolves by one of three moves. A hidden draw gets registered as its own node (B1's randomness lives in pair_idx, H1's in floor_shape). A gate becomes a branch of one conditional distribution whose off branch is a point mass (install is 0 without a click, spend is 0 for non-payers, rival zero always participates). A deterministic core times a noise draw is one conditional distribution with the core in the location parameter (the bid, the timestamp). The rule is: if a node seems mixed, the register is missing a hidden node, and the fix is to add that node, never to invent a fifth type.

## 2.1 Scope, pools, and generation order

**Pools.** T9Sim is a structured generative model over a single ad auction. Four fixed pools supply its entities: a user pool, an app pool and a campaign pool, and a rival pool of K = 8 persistent rival-bidder archetypes (latents LR1-LR6, frozen at creation). One auction row draws a (user, app, campaign, context) tuple, runs the conversion funnel, and settles the market against the rival pool.

**In and out of scope of the generator:**

- IN scope: the generative law producing the full uncensored master record (`auctions.parquet`), up to and including the last per-row assignment of step 10. Rows are written in blocks of `block_rows` = 65536 (`profiles.yaml`); blocking is an I/O convenience only, and every node's rng stream is positioned from (run seed, stream name, global row index), so the generated data is byte-identical under any block size. v2 inherits no v1 code, so the boundary is stated by step, not by a method name.
- OUT of scope: censoring into C1-C4 (`censor.py`, run after generation; semantics in 1.5), the learned models, and the bidder.

**Four pools.**

1. **User pool** (n^user users): latents LU1-LU6 + Section-A features. A1 region and A2 city are calibrated from **deduplicated** iPinYou users (user-population shape, not bid-weighted).
2. **App pool** (apps): visible `app_id` + `app_category`, plus latent `app_quality`, `app_audience_profile`.
3. **Campaign pool** (advertisers first, then their campaigns beneath them): internal `adv_tier` → visible `advertiser_id` → `advertiser_scale`, and `advertiser_id` → `campaign_id` → `ad_genre`, plus latent `creative_appeal`, `game_quality`. `c_idx` is downstream of all of these: it selects which campaign row an auction inherits, so it is never a parent of the C family.
4. **Rival pool**: K = 8 persistent rival-bidder archetypes, coded as 3 gaming + 5 non-gaming, each with latent private LTV-model loadings, retargeting matrix, budget-pacing state, exchange-participation profile, idiosyncratic dispersion and flight mask (LR1-LR6). Frozen at creation like the other pools; sampled per auction via participation draws.

**Auctions** (n rows): each samples one user + one app + one campaign, then draws the per-row context — exchange and slot (B3-B6) and time (D) — computes the true head means, and draws the rival-pool competing bid, bid/price labels (H) and funnel outcomes (E, F, G) on **every** row. Per-auction order:

1. Sample (user, app, campaign).
2. Draw the context: B3 ad_exchange and B6 slot_format, then the slot size drawn given that format and split into B4 width and B5 height, then D4 the week, then D2 hour and D3 day-of-week, and last the D1 timestamp assembled from hour, day and week.
3. Compute the four head means: p_i^click, p_i^install, p_i^pay, ℓ_i. Their product is v_i^⋆; its z-score is z_i^val.
4. Draw the two price shapes ψ^floor (`floor_shape`) and ψ^clearing (`pay_shape`), then compute H1 = ψ^floor · T^ecpm(B6) and β_0(x_i) (`base_e`) = max(ψ^clearing · T^ecpm(B6), 0.01 · T^ecpm(B6)). H1 inherits the iPinYou atom at zero, so about 57 percent of rows carry a zero floor; base_e is the shared price core every rival bid at step 6 is written around.
5. Participation draws A_ik, giving the participant set 𝒜_i and the count N_i = H9.
6. Rival bids B_ik, giving u_i = max_{k ∈ 𝒜_i} B_ik (LU7).
7. Our bid H2, which takes no input from LU7 (H2 and LU7 remain correlated through the shared latents).
8. w_i = 𝕀[b_i ≥ max(u_i, f_i)] (H3).
9. w_i^riv (`sold_lost`), then the winning price m_i = b_i if w_i, u_i if w_i^riv, ⊥ otherwise (H4; law in 2.4).
10. Funnel draws E, F, G on every row.

**Pool semantics.** Pools are built once. Each auction row picks an existing user, app and campaign rather than inventing new ones, so the same entity recurs across many rows carrying the latents it was created with. That repetition is what makes entity features learnable: `app_id` always arrives with the same hidden quality `LA1`. Only the context is redrawn per row: exchange, slot and time.

**Sampling weights.** Every user and every app is equally likely to be picked for an auction: no user is busier than another, and no app is more popular. Both refinements are deferred. Campaigns are the exception, with bigger advertisers bidding more often, following `advertiser_scale`.

**Rival persistence.** Rival archetypes persist across the whole 28-day window: retargeting rates are persistent, pacing follows a day-level AR(1) process, and flight schedules switch campaigns on and off. That persistence is exactly what makes their footprints statistically recoverable from rolling SSP aggregates (footprint rule, 2.5).

*Scoping note (Appendix M10): "drawn uniformly" is a statement about marginals; the IPF pairing tilts the (archetype × app) joint while preserving both marginals (uniform app popularity as column sums).*

## 2.2 Joint factorisation

**Completion to a full joint.**

```
P(everything) = P(pools) × Π over auctions i of P(row_i | pools)

P(pools) =
    Π users   P(LU1) P(A1) P(A2|A1) P(A3|LU1) P(A4|A3) P(A5|LU1) Π j=2..6 P(LUj|LU1) 1{user_id}
  × Π apps    1{B1 label, B2 quota} P(LA1) P(LA2|B2)
  × Π advertisers P(adv_tier) 1{C1, C2}
  × Π campaigns   1{C3} P(C4) P(LC1) P(LC2) 1{sample_weight}
  × Π rivals k=0..7 P(LR1_k) P(LR2_·k) P(LR3_k(·)) P(LR4_k·) P(LR5_k) P(LR6_k(·))

P(row | pools) =
    P(pair_idx) P(u_rows|pair_idx) 1{app_i, B1, B2 lookups}           -- 1 join
  × P(c_idx|user_vbin) 1{user_vbin, C3, C4 lookups}
  × P(B3) P(B6) P(_size|B6) 1{B4 = width of _size, B5 = height of _size}
  × P(D4) P(D2|LU1) P(D3|LU1) P(D1|D2,D3,D4)                          -- 2 context
  × 1{r_genre, m_stage, v_slot, ease, mu_cat, plat, t_pay}            -- 3 truth
  × 1{p_click, p_install, p_payer, e_ltv, ev_truth, z}
  × P(floor_shape) 1{H1} P(pay_shape) 1{base_e}                       -- 4 floor
  × Π k=0..7 P(participate_k | LR4, LR6, LR3, B3, day(D1))            -- 5 participation
  × Π participating k  P(b_k | base_e, z, LR1..LR3, LR5, A3, A5)      -- 6 rival bids
  × 1{LU7 = max b_k, H9 = count participate_k}
  × 1{eltv_b2} P(H2 | C2, B2, B6, _size)                               -- 7 our bid
  × 1{H3}  × 1{sold_lost, H4}                                         -- 8 win, 9 settle
  × P(E1|p_click) P(E2|E1,D1) P(F1|E1,p_install) P(F2|F1,E2)          -- 10 funnel
  × P(G1|F1,p_payer) P(G2|G1,mu_cat,LU5,LC2,plat) 1{G3, G4}
```

Section 2.1 gave the generator as a procedure: a list of steps in an order. This section gives the *same* generator as a single probability, $p(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{full}})$, the chance of producing one entire dataset: all four pools plus all $n$ auction rows. The quantity itself is never computed; it is written down for its **structure**, not its value, because that structure is what states exactly what depends on what, and it is what the censoring and identification results in Part III are read from.

The dataset is built in two stages, and the formula has one part for each. First the four **pools** are drawn: users, apps, campaigns, and a rival pool of $K=8$ rivals, each entity drawn once and independently. ($z^{u}_u$ is the whole latent bundle of user $u$, and likewise $z^{s}_a,z^{c}_c,z^{m}_k$.) Then each auction row $i$ is drawn, conditional on those pools.

$$
p(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{full}})=\underbrace{\prod_{u}p(z^{u}_u)\prod_{a}p(z^{s}_a)\prod_{c}p(z^{c}_c)\prod_{k}p(z^{m}_k)}_{p(z^{\mathrm{pool}})}\;\cdot\;\prod_{i}\Big[\underbrace{\prod_{j}p\!\left(x_{ij}\mid \mathrm{pa}(x_{ij})\right)}_{\text{pairing + context }x_i}\cdot\;p(y_i\mid x_i,z_i)\cdot\;p(h_i\mid z^{\mathrm{val}}_i,x_i,z_i,z^{m}_{1:K})\Big]
$$

**Reading the formula against the procedure.** Each factor is one part of the 2.1 procedure; the table below is the key. Reminder on the symbols: $\prod_u,\prod_a,\dots$ multiply the term over every user, app, and so on; $p(x\mid \mathrm{pa}(x))$ is the draw of $x$ given its *parents* $\mathrm{pa}(x)$, the variables it directly depends on.

| Factor | Procedure step | In words |
|---|---|---|
| $\prod_u p(z^{u}_u)\prod_a p(z^{s}_a)\prod_c p(z^{c}_c)\prod_k p(z^{m}_k)$ | before step 1 | The four pools, each entity drawn once and independently, before any auction. This is $p(z^{\mathrm{pool}})$. |
| outer $\prod_i[\dots]$ | "on every row" | One bracket per auction row $i$; rows are independent **given** the pools. |
| $\prod_j p(x_{ij}\mid \mathrm{pa}(x_{ij}))$ | 1-2 | Select the (user, app, campaign) triple and draw the context (time, slot, exchange) for row $i$. |
| $p(y_i\mid x_i,z_i)$ | 3, 10 | The true head means and the realised click/install/payer/spend outcomes. |
| $p(h_i\mid z^{\mathrm{val}}_i,x_i,z_i,z^{m}_{1:K})$ | 4-9 | Floor, rival participation and bids, our bid, win and the winning price. |

The two say the same thing with one difference: the procedure fixes dependency by *order* ("D2 and D3 first, then D1"), the formula by *parents* $\mathrm{pa}(x)$ — and only the parents form is order-free, so it states the structure directly.

*Notation.* $z_i=(z^{u}_{s^{u}(i)},z^{s}_{s^{a}(i)},z^{c}_{s^{c}(i)})$ is the latent bundle of the user, app and campaign row $i$ drew (indices `u_rows`, `app_i`, `c_idx`). Full parent sets are defined in the master table (1.6). *Reconciliation with the paper: the paper's $z_i$ has a fourth component, the rival pool $z^{m}$. Here $z_i$ is the row's user, app and campaign bundle only, and the rival bundle is carried separately as $z^{m}_{1:K}$ because it is not row indexed, so this specification's $z_i$ narrows the paper's.*

*Acyclicity.* $z^{\mathrm{val}}_i=(\log v^{\star}_i - \mu^{\mathrm{val}})/\sigma^{\mathrm{val}}$ is computed in the funnel/oracle block and read only by the market, never fed back, so each row is acyclic and the product is a valid joint distribution.

*The warm-up rule.* $\mu^{\mathrm{val}}$ and $\sigma^{\mathrm{val}}$ are fitted once on a separate warm-up sample drawn from its own random stream, not on the rows of the run, and only over rows with $v^{\star}_i > 0$. A row with $v^{\star}_i = 0$ receives $z^{\mathrm{val}}_i = 0$ rather than a standardised value. Inactive users have zero value and are 30 percent of the archetype mix, so about 30 percent of rows carry no value tilt into the rival bid. That is intended, rivals do not bid up worthless impressions, and it is stated here so it is not read as a rounding artefact.

## 2.5 The Rival-Pool Layer (the Private-Rival Market)

**Purpose.** Rival behaviour is modelled as explicit structure (retargeting, private LTV models, pacing, flights, exchange participation, competition density), not one iid noise term. As noise the competing bid `LU7` would be fully DSP-predictable, with no hidden signal in the SSP price. Under conditionally independent private values (Athey and Haile 2002), one clearing price adds no conditioning power. Modelled explicitly, `LU7` is the max of rival bids carrying value-correlated components the DSP cannot reconstruct, which is the footprint SSP data recovers.

**The five modelled mechanisms:**

| # | Mechanism modelled | How it is generated |
|---|---|---|
| a | Rivals' own retargeting lists / user-ID methods | `R_{s(i),k}`: per (observable segment, rival) retargeting matrix (LR2) |
| b | Rivals' own LTV models | `ω_k·z_i^val`: private loading on the latent impression value (LR1) |
| c | Budget-pacing state, campaign flight schedules | `p_k(d_i)` AR(1) pacing (LR3) + `F_k(d_i)` flight masks (LR6) |
| d | Ad-exchange dynamics | `π_k(e_i)`: per-rival exchange participation (LR4) |
| e | Competition density | endogenous N_i → **H9** |

An earlier form of this line deferred to a guard in 1.6 that 1.6 states is unnecessary.
- Archetype k=0 is forced always-on, so 𝒜_i ≠ {}, N_i ∈ {1, …, 8} and u_i > 0 on every auction.
- **The day index d_i is anchored to the start of the 28-day window, never to the first row of whatever chunk is in memory** (open item O3 3e). **The footprint rule.** No view ever sees LR1-LR6; what C3/C4 can recover is their statistical footprint. The level components (retargeting, pacing, participation, flights) leave cell-level traces in winning prices (H4) and competition density (H9) over observable cells (time, exchange, segment); LR5 noise leaves none by construction. The latent-value interaction ω_k·z_i^val is recoverable by no one: it prices a latent impression value that no side observes, supplying realism and DSP-unpredictability rather than SSP-recoverable signal. No view is ever handed a rival-segment feature directly; H4/H9 traces are the only channel. The Tier-2 features that read those traces are pipeline, not generator (entry V-8, 2.6).

**Calibration and gates.** Calibrated by win-rate autocalibration and the validation targets and direction checks; no moment raking.

**Anchoring ρ (external, not tuned).** The anchor is the value of lost-price information: a published censored-vs-full-information winning-price comparison (Wang et al. 2023) implies a 5-13% all-rows price-RMSE gain from observing lost prices at ≈ 30% win rate. The v1 ρ-grid maps this monotonically onto ρ^anchor ≈ 0.8, that published spread carrying through to a band ρ ∈ [0.6, 0.9]; v2 fixes the dial at the anchored value for every run (O6). The simulator's ≈ 30% win rate is an instrumentation choice (enough won rows at feasible scale); real DSP win rates are typically lower, making measured SSP value conservative.

# Part III - Identification propositions (P1-P4)

**What this part is for.** The C1-C4 comparison produces numbers. This part says why those numbers
mean something: it states what each view can learn **in principle**, with unlimited data and a perfect
model. Without it, a gap between C1 and C3 is just an output of this simulator. With it, the gap is a
consequence of what each view can and cannot see.

**How to read it.** Each proposition opens in plain words, then repeats itself formally. The plain
opening is the claim; the formal statement is the same claim written so it can be checked. Read the
openings first. If you only read the openings you will still have the argument.

**The four in one line each.**

| | Claim |
|---|---|
| **P1** | Every view can learn the win curve. SSP data makes that faster, not possible |
| **P2** | Only C3 and C4 can learn how many rivals showed up. This is the real SSP gain |
| **P3** | C1 and C3 see the funnel only on auctions they won, which biases them, and no reweighting fixes it |
| **P4** | All of it depends on rho. In a market where rivals ignore true value, SSP data is worth nothing |

*The views are the deterministic masks 𝒞^k of the master law defined in the Censoring Map (1.5). These are proof sketches, not full proofs.*

---

## The ground rules

Three conventions the propositions rely on. They are here rather than inside each proposition so the
arguments stay short.

**What "identified" means here.** A thing is identified in a view if the data that view sees could
only have come from one value of it. If two different markets would produce identical data in that
view, nothing in that view can tell them apart, and the thing is not identified. This is always
relative to a stated class of candidate markets: **𝒫**, the generators that share this document's
funnel and context block (Parts I-II) but allow any market mechanism measurable in row context,
z_i^val, day, exchange and segment. Two members of 𝒫 are **observationally equivalent** in view 𝒞^k
if they induce the same joint law of 𝒞^k(𝒟^full). A functional is **identified** in 𝒞^k if it takes
one value on each equivalence class.

**Rows are exchangeable, not independent.** The pools are frozen and rivals share within-day pacing
states, so rows are not iid. With unlimited rows from a single 28-day window, what is estimated
consistently is the pool-conditional version of each functional.

**Notation.** x_i is the always-visible pre-bid columns (A, B, `C`, D, f_i); b_i (H2) and w_i (H3)
are carried as separate symbols; u_i = LU7. The 2.7 symbols apply, including its four paper
reconciliations: m_i here is the H4 winning price and the threshold is m_i^win. 𝒞^k applies the
paper's two field-level operators, the outcome operator and the price operator, jointly and at table
level, with the paper's reporting-status argument held fixed because no delayed-reporting censoring
is modelled.

---

## P1. Every view can learn the win curve

**Plainly.** The win curve says how likely you are to win at each bid. Every view can learn it, and
none has an advantage. The reason is that your own bid is randomly jittered by the exploration noise,
so every auction is a small experiment: you bid something, and you either win or you do not. Enough
of those experiments trace the whole curve without ever seeing a price.

Seeing real prices, which only C3 and C4 do, gets you the same curve from fewer rows. It does not get
you a different curve. **So on this quantity SSP data buys speed, not capability.**

One limit applies to everyone. Below the floor price nothing can be learned in any view, because no
bid wins there and nothing is observed. That costs nothing, since no bidding decision depends on that
region.

*Formal statement.* In every condition C1-C4, the win curve b ↦ F_{u|x}(b) = Pr(u_i ≤ b | x_i) is identified for almost every b ≥ f_i, hence at every b ≥ f_i by monotonicity and right-continuity. Below the floor it is identified in no condition. Pooling floor levels extends identification down to the essential infimum of the floor's conditional support. F_{u|x}(b) is the paper's F̂_m(b | x_i) conditioned on the top rival bid rather than on the price, so the two agree for b ≥ f_i and differ below the floor.

*Sketch.* b_i = v_i^proxy(x_i) · ζ^shade · η_i, η_i ∼ LogNormal(0, σ^explore), and the exploration factor is independent of u_i given x_i. So each row is a randomised threshold query of u_i's conditional law: Pr(w_i = 1 | x_i, b_i = b) = F_{u|x}(b) for b ≥ f_i. The lognormal has support (0, ∞), so the queries trace the whole curve above the floor. Below f_i no bid wins regardless of u_i, and C3/C4's m_i is ⊥ on unsold rows, so no condition observes that region (and no bid decision depends on it). w_i is visible in every condition, so no condition has an identification advantage for the win curve. C3/C4's exact prices (m_i = u_i on sold-lost rows) add only **statistical efficiency**: direct observation versus threshold reconstruction through the thin exploration tails.

---

## P2. Only C3 and C4 can learn how many rivals showed up

**Plainly.** In C1 and C2 you cannot tell how many rivals you were bidding against. A market with
eight rivals and a market with a single rival who bids the maximum of eight produce **identical data**
in those views. Nothing in them can separate the two, so the participation process is not learnable
there at all, no matter how much data you have.

C3 and C4 see the rival count as a column, so for them it is learnable immediately.

**This is the one place SSP data adds a capability rather than speed**, and it is where the measured
C3 minus C1 contrast comes from.

There is a limit even in C3 and C4. They learn the aggregate: how many rivals bid, and what prices
cleared in a given cell of time, exchange and segment. They never learn which particular rival did
what, whose bid was the maximum, or any individual rival's pacing. That stays hidden from everyone,
by design.

*Formal statement.* The participation law Pr(N_i = n_0 | x_i) (H9) is identified only in C3/C4. In C1/C2, any two members of 𝒫 that induce the same joint conditional law of the u_i-process given the context-funnel block (x_{1:n}, z_{1:n}^val) are observationally equivalent. So K, the participation process, and the per-rival decomposition of u_i are not identified there. Even in C3/C4, only the cell-level participation and winning-price laws (the observable footprints) become identified. The per-rival decomposition (which rival participated, whose bid is the max, individual pacing paths) stays unidentified.

*Sketch.* In C1/C2 the observables touch the market only through per-row threshold queries of u_i (w_i); m_i and N_i are masked. Construction (a member of 𝒫 outside the parametric bid family, which is why equivalence is defined over 𝒫): a single always-on pseudo-rival that simulates the K = 8 pool internally and bids its max. By construction u_i and all its couplings to (x_i, z_i^val, day, exchange, segment) are unchanged, while N_i ≡ 1. So N_i is not identified, nor is anything finer than the u_i-process law. In C3/C4, H9 is a column, so its conditional law is identified trivially; jointly with m_i, the cell-level winning-price distributions over observable cells (time × exchange × segment) are identified too.

---

## P3. C1 and C3 are biased by what they won, and reweighting cannot fix it

**Plainly.** This is the MMP proposition, and the formal version of what this project calls the DSP's
biased view.

C1 and C3 only see whether a user clicked, installed or paid on auctions they **won**. They won those
auctions for reasons connected to the users' hidden value, which is the same hidden value that drives
whether those users convert. So the rows they can learn from are not a fair sample of the rows they
have to predict on.

The usual fix is to reweight the won rows by how likely each was to be won. **That does not work
here**, because the thing causing the selection is not in the data. No weighting scheme built from
observable columns can undo it. This is why the cross-fitted IPW arm recovered none of the lost-row
truth: it was not an implementation failure, it was the proposition.

There is a technical escape hatch, and it is worth understanding why it does not help. If you bid
high enough you win almost regardless, so extremely high bids give unbiased rows. In the limit the
truth is recoverable. But those bids almost never happen, so the useful data in that channel shrinks
to nothing at any real scale. Identification exists; usable identification does not.

C2 and C4 see the funnel on every row, won or lost, so none of this applies. **Their gain is
regularity, not existence: they make estimable what was already identified.**

*Formal statement.* (i) In C1/C3, funnel outcomes y_i^{(stage)}, stage ∈ {click, install, pay, ltv}, are not independent of selection given observables: y_i^{(stage)} is not independent of w_i given (x_i, b_i). So no observable propensity is a balancing score, and reweighting or regression on observables (IPW and kin) is inconsistent for 𝔼[y_i^{(stage)} | x_i]. (ii) 𝔼[y_i^{(stage)} | x_i] is still identified in C1/C3, but only through the exploration tail (identification at infinity). That channel is irregular: it has no regular (root-n) estimator and carries no usable information at any feasible scale. (iii) C2/C4 upgrade 𝔼[y_i^{(stage)} | x_i] to directly identified, regular status, because y_i^{(stage)} is observed on all rows.

*Sketch.* Selection w_i depends on u_i, and u_i loads on the latent value z_i^val through ρ · ω_k — the same latents that drive y_i^{(stage)}'s conditional means (the p_i^{(stage)} heads). Conditioning on (x_i, b_i) does not block that path. That is (i): the DSP's biased view (selection bias) as a formal non-ignorability claim. For (ii): Pr(w_i = 1 | x_i, b_i = b, latents) → 1 as b → ∞ (full-support exploration, the same instrument as P1). So 𝔼[y_i^{(stage)} | x_i, b_i = b, w_i = 1] → 𝔼[y_i^{(stage)} | x_i] by dominated convergence. Identification holds in the limit, but the identifying mass vanishes in the tail, so the channel is irregular. P1 and P3 are therefore consistent: the MMP's gain is a **regularity** gain, not existence-of-identification.

## P4. Whether SSP data is worth anything depends on rho

**Plainly.** Rho controls how much each rival's bid leans on what the impression is actually worth.

At rho = 0 rivals bid without regard to true value. The price then carries no information about value
beyond what you could already see, so **SSP data is worth nothing** and the market is the classic
independent-private-values case. That is the null this project contrasts against.

Above zero, each rival's bid leans on the true value, so the winning price becomes a noisy
measurement of it, and a view that sees prices sees something a view without them cannot reconstruct.

**So the SSP result is not a general claim about SSP data. It is a claim about markets where rivals'
valuations move together**, which is why rho is anchored to a published band rather than chosen.

One honest limit: that the effect grows steadily as rho rises is **proved for a single rival and only
observed for the max of eight**. The argmax can switch between rivals as rho grows, which is why
monotonicity is left as a conjecture rather than claimed as a theorem.

*Formal statement.* At ρ = 0, conditional on x_i and β_0(x_i), rival bids carry no term in the latent value z_i^val. This is the independent-private-values (IPV) case, conditional on x_i and β_0(x_i) — the Athey-Haile conditional-IPV setting. Then (m_i, N_i) are conditionally independent of (z_i^val, y_i^{(stage)}) given (x_i, β_0(x_i)), so SSP data carries no information about any value functional beyond x_i. For ρ > 0, Cov(log u_i, z_i^val | x_i) > 0, and m_i on sold-lost rows is a noisy measurement of z_i^val. So the SSP view refines the value-relevant information. The covariance is exactly linear in ρ for K = 1; for the max-of-K market, its monotonicity in ρ is empirically established (conjectured, not proved).

*Sketch.* At ρ = 0, log B_ik = log β_0(x_i) + σ_k·ε_ik, so the max u_i and the count N_i are functions of quantities independent of z_i^val given (x_i, β_0(x_i)). For ρ > 0: every ω_k ≥ 0 and the always-on rival k = 0 has ω_0 > 0, so log u_i is pointwise nondecreasing in the z_i^val realisation. With Var(z_i^val | x_i) > 0, the association (FKG/Chebyshev) inequality gives Cov(log u_i, z_i^val | x_i) > 0. For K = 1 the covariance is ρ · ω_0 · Var(z_i^val | x_i), linear in ρ. For the max, the argmax can switch between rivals as ρ grows, so monotonicity is left as an empirically supported conjecture.

*The value-awareness dial.* γ^riv scales the latent coupling ω_k · z_i^val, which is measurable neither in x_i nor in the SSP's persistent footprints (the footprint rule). It inflates irreducible log-bid variance in C1 and C3 together, so it cannot create an SSP-only increment. Empirically it is flat for γ^riv ≤ 1 (+0.0253 / +0.0253 / +0.0254 at γ^riv = 0 / 0.5 / 1, CLF) and mildly compressive beyond (+0.0237 / +0.0224 at γ^riv = 1.5 / 2).

### 3b Archetype tilt strength — DECIDED, invent and declare

O1 wired archetype into os, device type and day of week. The rake already pins each marginal, so the only free quantity is how far an archetype pulls away from it. No outside dataset can supply that number, because archetype is T9's own latent and no public source has an archetype-by-os cell. So the values are invented, declared `inferred`, and swept.

**The form, which matters more than the numbers.** Each table is one dial, not a hand-written grid of cells.

$$P(\text{child}=c \mid \text{archetype}=a) \;=\; \operatorname{logistic}\!\big(\operatorname{logit}(p^{\text{marginal}}) \;+\; \tau \cdot s_a\big)$$

`s_a` is a single archetype ladder shared by all three tables, being the monetisation order whale, engaged spender, casual, time filler, inactive, scored +2 down to −2, then centred on the archetype shares and scaled to unit variance under them. Three properties earn this form.

- **The null is exact.** τ = 0 reproduces a parentless draw bit for bit, so O1 is reversible at run time rather than by deleting tables, and the sensitivity sweep has a true zero point.
- **The rake stays honest.** Because the ladder is centred on the shares, the pre-rake marginal already lands within 0.6 percent of target. The rake is then correcting rounding, not undoing the tilt. An uncentred ladder would leave the rake fighting the dial, and the reported tilt would not be the tilt that ran.
- **One auditable number per table** rather than 10 cells that can drift apart.

**The values.**

| Table | Tilted on | τ | Resulting spread, top to bottom archetype | Relative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A3 os given archetype | P(iOS) | 0.13 | 0.677 down to 0.554, around a 0.59 marginal | 1.22× |
| A5 device type given archetype | P(tablet) | 0.13 | 0.139 down to 0.088, around a 0.10 marginal | 1.59× |
| D3 day of week given archetype | P(weekend) | 0.04 | 0.310 down to 0.277, around a 0.286 marginal | 1.12× |

**Why these strengths.** The os dial is set so the top archetype reaches 0.68 iOS against a 0.59 population marginal. The device dial takes the same τ deliberately, since a shared strength on the same ladder is easier to defend than two unrelated numbers, and the minority class turns it into a smaller absolute swing on its own.

## O13 The two encoder shrink constants — DECIDED, collapse to one

**Raised and settled 15 August 2026.** The gate-1 provenance sweep found that v1 had ONE constant,
`HCP_EB_K = 20.0`, consumed by both historical-price encoders, while an early v2 draft had split it into
`encoders.dsp.shrink` and `encoders.ssp.shrink`.

**Ken's verdict: collapse to one.** `training.yaml` now carries a single `encoders.shrink` at 20.0, marked
`shared_by: [dsp, ssp]`. The split is reversed and must not be reintroduced.

**Why one is the principled choice, not merely the simpler one.** Empirical-Bayes shrinkage already adapts
to sample size by construction. The estimate is

$$\text{EB mean} \;=\; \frac{\text{cell\_sum} + k \cdot \text{parent}}{\text{cell\_count} + k}$$

so a cell with more observations is automatically shrunk less. The SSP encoder does see more rows than the
DSP encoder, since it averages over won and sold-lost rows rather than won rows alone, but `cell_count`
already absorbs that. A second `k` would be correcting for something the formula has corrected for.

**What two would have cost.** Two independently settable constants place a free knob exactly where the
C3 minus C1 result lives. Moving `ssp.shrink` alone could manufacture or erase the SSP contrast, and
nothing in the design would object. Collapsing removes that degree of freedom, which matters more here
than in most places because this contrast is the headline.

**The asymmetry that remains, and belongs.** The 2 encoders differ in what each averages. The DSP encoder
takes won-row own bids, carries the DSP's biased view and is served to all 4 views. The SSP encoder takes
winning prices over won and sold-lost rows, is unbiased, and is served to C3 and C4 only. That difference
is the entire C3 minus C1 mechanism and it needs no second dial to express it.

**The diagnostic survives.** v1 exposed this constant as its leak-versus-debias probe, sweepable through
the environment variable `T9_HCP_EB_K`, to test whether the C3 minus C1 gain is real de-biasing or cell
memorisation. One shared dial is all that probe needs, so nothing is lost by collapsing.

## O6 The rho dial moves variance as well as correlation — CLOSED, does not arise in v2

**Ken's verdict, 15 August 2026: take rho = 0.8 from v1 and do not sweep it.** The value is already in
place at `profiles.yaml` `default.rho` (O3 3d gave it its one home). Nothing in the generator changes.

**The mechanism, recorded so it is not rediscovered.** Under the max over K rivals, rho scales the
structured components directly and is not variance-raked, so total log-bid variance grows with rho. It
therefore moves 2 quantities at once: how closely the competing price tracks the row's true value, which
is the intended effect and what P3 states as Cov(log u_i, z^val_i | x_i) > 0, and how widely the price
distribution spreads, which is not intended. Both make an observed price more useful to a model. v1
absorbed only the level consequence, through the `k_global` win-rate pin, which leaves the dispersion
untouched.

**Why it does not arise in v2.** The confound exists only while rho **varies**. v2 generates 30 datasets
at one fixed rho and the rebuild plan contains no dial sweep of any kind, so no v2 claim rests on
attributing a change to correlation. At a single rho the market simply has the correlation and the
dispersion it has, both being real properties of it, and the C1 to C4 ablation holds everything but
observability constant.

**Why the choice of rho = 0.8 is unaffected.** It was anchored on an outcome, not on a mechanism.
`docs/v10_Anchor_Bands.md` sets it so the simulated contrast matches the published band in
Wang et al. 2023, and matching the size of a real market's SSP value does not depend on which of the 2
quantities produced it.

**What this leaves with v1, and how big it turned out to be.** The confound attaches to v1's rho sweep
and to the two-dial identification claim built on it, "rho moves the contrast, value-awareness does not"
(`docs/KDD_Schema_Decision_10Jul2026.md:111`). It is recorded as a v1 limitation in
`docs/v1/Known_Defects_Register.md` defect 3e, not carried into v2.

It is also **bounded**, because the sweep measured both quantities and nobody had read them together.
`docs/results/sweep_rho_v10_100k.json` gives, over rho from 0 to 1, a **3.4-fold** rise in
corr(log u, z^val) against only a **13.5 percent** rise in sd(log u), while the SSP contrast rises
**11-fold** and the win rate stays pinned at 0.302. At rho = 0 dispersion is already near its final
value while the contrast sits at its floor. So dispersion without correlation buys almost nothing, and
the confound, while real, is not what moves the result.

**If a v2 sweep is ever wanted**, the way to earn the claim is to hold total log-bid variance fixed while
varying rho, so correlation moves alone. That was never run, in v1 or v2, and it is not free: rho scales
3 terms at once, so holding variance constant means scaling something else down and it has not been shown
that this can be done without disturbing the quantity being measured. Design it as an experiment, not a
switch.

## O7 Probability calibration is measured, not fitted — DECIDED, fit nothing

The submitted paper states that each view and prediction head is calibrated on a validation split using isotonic regression or Platt scaling. v1's code never fits a calibrator, it measures ECE and reports it.

**Ken's verdict, 14 August 2026. v2 fits no calibrator.** It measures ECE per head per view exactly as v1 did, and the paper's method sentence is amended to say calibration is measured rather than fitted. Nothing in the build changes.

**Why.** Calibration cannot move a single AUC, because every method that fits one is monotonic and monotonic maps do not reorder rows. The headline result of this project is an AUC contrast, so calibration has no upside on it and can only change the economic figures.

Against that, the cost is not shared evenly across the 4 views. A calibrator has to be fitted on data the model has not seen, which means carving a slice out of the training days. C2 and C4 observe conversions on every row while C1 and C3 observe them on won rows only, so the same slice costs C1 and C3 far more, at roughly 70 payer positives against 500 at 10M. Thinning the training data of exactly the views the ablation compares risks moving the contrast for a reason that has nothing to do with data-layer value. Isotonic regression makes this worse, since a staircase fitted on 70 positives fits noise, so the thin views would need Platt while the others took isotonic, and the 4 views would stop being treated identically inside an ablation whose premise is that they are.

**The evidence this rests on.** v1 measured the problem rather than assuming it, and the numbers are mixed rather than uniformly benign.

| Head | Measured ECE | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| click | 0.003 to 0.005 | effectively calibrated |
| install, won rows | 0.012 to 0.016 | acceptable |
| install, all rows | 0.038 to 0.060 | real miscalibration |
| install, lost rows | 0.047 to 0.074 | the worst case |
| win, AFT bidder | about 0.052 | real miscalibration |

**Rejected reasoning, recorded so it is not re-argued.** Running 10 seeds does not substitute for calibration. Seeds average variance, not bias. If every seed's install head is overconfident by the same amount, averaging 10 of them gives a precise estimate of a shifted number rather than an unshifted one.

**The residual risk, accepted knowingly.** Install ECE is worst on lost rows at 0.074, and lost rows are where the censoring story lives. The economic contrast is partly protected because a bias shared across all 4 views largely cancels in a difference, but how much it cancels has not been measured. If the v2 economics look anomalous, this is the first thing to check.

Affects the Tier-1 heads, the reported ECE, and one sentence of the paper's method description.

## O8 The win classifier is not monotone-constrained — DECIDED, add the constraint

![Why the win classifier needs a monotone constraint: a small sag in the fitted win curve moves the profit-maximising bid.](C:/Users/ktaiw/Desktop/MSc%20Project/Schema%20diagrams/O8_monotone_win_curve.png)

*Figure O8. The same sag, seen twice. On the win curve it looks minor. On expected profit, which is what the bidder maximises, it moves the chosen bid from \$0.027 to \$0.037 and forgoes 14 percent of the profit. Curves computed by `t9v2/tools/make_o8_diagram.py` from the project's own win-curve form; illustrative, not a measurement of v1.*

The paper describes the Tier-2 model as a monotone win classifier. v1's XGBoost parameters carry no monotone constraint, so the description was of the model the project meant to build rather than the one it ran.

**Ken's verdict, 15 August 2026. v2 adds the constraint.** `training.yaml` `xgboost.tier2_win_classifier` now carries `monotone_constraints: {bid_price: 1}`, forcing the fitted p(win given bid) to be non-decreasing in the bid.

**Why it is a fact rather than a preference.** You win an auction by outbidding the field, so raising your own bid cannot reduce your chance of winning. That is a property of the mechanism, not an assumption about the data. XGBoost does not know it is looking at an auction, though: it fits whatever the training rows suggest, and where the rows are thin at some bid level, noise can make bidding more look like winning less. Nothing in the algorithm objects.

**Why a small sag matters more than it looks.** The bidder does not choose the highest win probability, it maximises (ev minus b) times p(win given b) over the 48-price grid. Figure O8 shows the same sag twice: barely visible on the win curve, and punched straight through the peak of the profit curve, moving the chosen bid and forgoing about 14 percent of the profit on that impression. The grid is only 48 log-spaced prices, so a sag can also sit between two of them and never be seen directly.

**How the constraint works.** The setting names the features the prediction must be non-decreasing in. When a tree splits on `bid_price`, the higher-bid branch is forced to a value at least that of the lower-bid branch, and the bounds carry down the tree, so monotonicity holds by construction rather than by luck. The key is written by feature **name**, not by position, so it cannot silently attach to the wrong column if the Tier-2 feature list changes at stage 4.

**What to expect at stage 4, so it is not mistaken for a regression.** The constraint removes the model's freedom to fit noise in one direction, so a small drop in raw win AUC is expected. The measure that matters is whether the bidder's chosen bid improves, not whether the classifier's own score does.

**Deliberately not decided.** `floor_price` is the other feature the win probability arguably moves monotonically in, downward, since a higher reserve is harder to clear at a fixed bid. Ken decided the bid constraint only, so a −1 on `floor_price` remains a separate question rather than being folded in here.

Affects section 2.5's settlement and the Tier-2 contract.

## O11 The switched-off variants are dropped — DECIDED, drop them

v1 carried an on/off flag for each of the 6 dependencies, being pairing, exposure, os_spend, payer_timing, hour and `rival_pool`. The flags began as a way to compare a new dependency against the older independent generator without keeping 2 codebases, and they later became the mechanism-off sensitivity test. The consequence is that every node had to declare a second law for the switched-off case, which is where the register's compound types come from, such as `T4 (paper); T1 uniform when OFF`, and why this document had to say that type is a property of the pair (node, configuration) rather than of the node.

**Ken's verdict, 14 August 2026. v2 has no switched-off variants.** The 6 dependencies are fixed properties of the design. Every node carries exactly one law, type is a property of the node, and there is no configuration in which a dependency is absent.

**Why.** v1's checked-in default had every flag off while the paper ran them all on, so both paths had to work. v2 inverts that, since the operative design is all 6 on. With the negative control also dropped, nothing in v2 would ever run with a dependency switched off, so the off variants would be written, wired and tested without once being executed.

**What this removes.** The 8 variant-only rows drop out of the register's census, and no declared-absent rows are left, since O1 wired the last 3 probability tables on 15 August 2026. The compound type entries collapse to single types. Nodes stop carrying a variants map. v1's all-OFF byte-identity gate and its generator-OFF leak gate do not carry over, since there is no OFF configuration to compare against. `bid_density`, the `la2_*` columns and the rival machinery become unconditional, so those columns are always present in the master.

**What this costs.** A mechanism-off sensitivity test can no longer be produced by changing a setting. Producing one would mean writing code. Accepted, because v1 already established that the rival-pool design produces the C3 minus C1 gap, and the v8 record holds the iid-market null.

Affects 2.0's flags bullet and type taxonomy, O5's absences, the register's variants map, and the `default` profile in the rebuild plan.
